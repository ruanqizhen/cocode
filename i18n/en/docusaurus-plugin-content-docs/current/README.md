# To My Future Self in the AI Era

> "Stay hungry, stay foolish." — Stewart Brand

The recent advancements in AI have truly astounded me. It feels like an approaching tsunami; the closer I look, the more anxious I become.

Being in the IT industry—a field notoriously considered a "young man's game"—I've always harbored a strong sense of career anxiety. This industry evolves at a breakneck pace; you could be the company's rockstar today and on the chopping block for "optimization" tomorrow. Years ago, I realized I couldn't possibly survive in this industry until retirement, and I seriously started pondering my fallback plans for when I eventually lost my job.

Two years ago, I still felt I had a few viable paths forward. I could become a tech instructor, teaching junior engineers or even running coding bootcamps for K-12 students. After all, having navigated tech giants for years, I had accumulated solid experience. At the time, the entire industry was caught up in a "learn to code" frenzy; it seemed that as long as you could write code, you'd never run out of students to teach.

Another option was writing. I used to submit technical articles to magazines and even earned royalties from them. I figured that even if I stopped coding, I could make a living through my words. A programmer transitioning to a writer sounded somewhat romantic—researching technology by day and writing by night, becoming a cyberpunk wordsmith for the internet age.

Unfortunately, the rapid rise of AI has effectively neutralized all the fallback plans I had envisioned. The squeeze AI has put on the demand for software engineers is already painfully obvious. Work that once required a team of engineers collaborating for weeks can now be completed by a single person using AI in just a few days. AI isn't merely replacing "people who write code"; it's replacing "people who teach others how to write code." If the code of the future is primarily generated by AI, how many people will still want to learn programming?

The writing industry experienced this shock even earlier. Over the past year, all I've heard about is the shrinking publishing industry, plummeting royalties, and the total reshuffling of independent media. In the past, a writer's biggest competitors were their peers; today, their biggest competitor is a Large Language Model that is online 24/7, never tires, and doesn't demand health insurance. AI can generate a well-structured, eloquently phrased, and emotionally resonant article in seconds. Consequently, many people have stopped buying books and waiting years for an author to polish a manuscript, instead turning to AI to generate exactly what they want on the spot.

It is a deeply absurd feeling: those of us who once made a living off "knowledge" and "creativity" suddenly find that the first thing to be industrialized is creativity itself. If one day I truly lose my job, there probably won't be such a thing as "changing careers"; it will simply be called retirement. (Well, it seems my previous pessimistic expectations were wrong; I really am going to work in the software industry until I "retire.")

But the problem is, I am nowhere near retirement age. Thinking it over, the most realistic plan is to simply try not to become obsolete. Survive one more day if I can. Since the wave is already here, I might as well learn how to surf.

So, I forced myself to dive deep into AI programming, research autonomous Agents, and explore the endless stream of new tools, paradigms, and workflows. But the more I learn, the stronger my sense of vertigo becomes. This era is changing too fast. Concepts I barely grasped yesterday are already obsolete today; a revolutionary framework released in the morning might have a "next-generation replacement" proposed by the evening.

It doesn't feel like I'm learning technology; it feels like technology is desperately trying to leave me behind. This realization has sapped much of my recent enthusiasm for learning. It's not out of laziness, but rather the instinctive sense of powerlessness humans feel when faced with exponential change. You gear up to earnestly climb a mountain, only to discover the entire tectonic plate is shifting beneath your feet.

Yet, rationality tells me that anxiety is pointless. Technological waves won't halt out of fear, nor will they decelerate out of nostalgia. Instead of endlessly agonizing over "whether AI will replace me," it's far better to dive in and see exactly what it's capable of.

I am someone who needs a clear driving force to truly buckle down and learn. Ultimately, I decided to write another book. Not to teach others, but to leave a record for myself. To document how I relearn, readapt, and redefine what it means to be a "programmer" in this chaotic, crazy, and constantly shifting era.

What you are reading now is the beginning of that record.

## What This Book Covers

This book primarily documents the genuine shock and transformation experienced by an ordinary programmer when AI begins to deeply intervene in the act of "creation."

Of course, it will also include many practical methods for leveraging AI. These techniques won't be strictly limited to "programming." Because AI is making coding increasingly cheap, it is beginning to seep into our everyday lives like water and electricity, rapidly blurring the boundaries of what programming actually is. Many people with zero technical background have started controlling software, generating content, and automating their workflows using natural language. In a sense, everyone in the future will "learn to program" in one form or another.

The pace of AI's development is almost frightening. Many technical details, tool names, and workflows mentioned in this book may become obsolete within months; things that look cutting-edge today might be considered jokes tomorrow. I will continuously iterate and update the content to keep it as relevant as possible.

If you have any thoughts, opinions, or discover better tools or more interesting directions while reading, please reach out. In an era where the speed of change outpaces individual cognition, no one can truly claim to have "mastered" anything.

## How This Started

Five years ago, I open-sourced my first book on GitHub. Back then, I manually crafted a website using JavaScript from scratch. Later, when I discovered mature e-book generators, I thought *that* was the peak of convenience.

Three years ago, I wrote a book on Python programming. That time, AI showed me what true convenience looked like: a draft that would have traditionally taken three months was finished in less than one with AI assistance. Even then, I vaguely felt a shift happening.

By the time I started writing *this* book, the world had changed once again. The speed of this transition was so jarring it left me a bit dazed. From the moment I made the decision to write it to the time the draft went live online, it took less than an hour.

The entire process went roughly like this:

- First, I made the decision: I would force myself to systematically learn AI programming through the act of "writing a book."
- I asked an AI: "If I want to truly master AI programming, what technologies, tools, and paradigms should I learn?"
- The AI quickly generated a comprehensive study curriculum.
- Next, I asked the AI to generate a book's table of contents based on this curriculum.
- Then, I opened an AI coding environment and prompted it: "Create a Docusaurus-based e-book project and integrate the table of contents we just generated."
- Minutes later, a fully structured, paginated, and functional e-book project materialized on my computer.
- I opened GitHub and created a repository.
- Finally, I connected the repository to Cloudflare Pages and deployed it.
- Minutes later, this book was officially live.

When the AI generated a perfectly presentable e-book in just a few minutes, that familiar sense of vertigo returned. I couldn't help but wonder: if AI can already accomplish these tasks so effortlessly, is what I'm doing still meaningful?

I had to repeatedly remind myself: the purpose of this book is to force me to keep learning, keep thinking, and keep pace with this era. That thought alone is what kept me writing.

Strictly speaking, many of the steps I just outlined no longer even require switching between different applications. Today's AI programming tools are evolving into "unified workbenches." Planning, generating, coding, deploying, modifying, debugging—processes that previously demanded a collaborative team can now be executed by one person staring at a chat interface.

In the chapters that follow, I will share the exact commands, prompts, and actual conversational transcripts I used to create this book, allowing readers to fully reproduce the process.

To be clear, publishing an AI-generated draft isn't something to be proud of. The real achievement isn't "generating a book in a few minutes." The real challenge is: in the days ahead, can I replace this AI-generated boilerplate with my own original insights through continuous learning, practice, and reflection?

Because only the knowledge that you have truly digested, wrestled with, doubted, and rebuilt from the ground up can ever be considered your own.
