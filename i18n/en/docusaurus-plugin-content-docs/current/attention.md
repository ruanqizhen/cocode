# Epilogue

> Attention Is All You Need

What I want to discuss is not that famous paper, but our human attention.

For a long time in the past, the greatest contradiction in human society was the lack of information. Books were expensive, newspapers and periodicals were limited, and knowledge was in the hands of a few. If an ordinary person wanted to learn knowledge in a certain field, they often had to spend a lot of time looking for materials. The Internet twenty years ago was so exciting largely because it broke this scarcity. For the first time, people could access massive amounts of information so easily.

At that time, the most discussed problem was "can't find it." But today, the problem we face has become "can't turn it off." Information floods in from all directions like a deluge. News, short videos, live streams, social media, recommendation feeds, AI-generated content—countless pieces of information scramble to occupy our screens. Once-expensive content is becoming extremely cheap, yet the time humans can invest hasn't increased by a single fraction.

The emergence of AI has pushed this trend to a new stage. In the past, behind an article, a picture, or a video, there was at least one person who had to invest time and energy. Now, the cost of generating these contents is rapidly approaching zero. With the help of AI, a single person can accomplish work that previously required a whole team. Content production is no longer the bottleneck; attention is.


Continuously refreshing information feeds gives people the illusion that they are acquiring knowledge, but most of the time, we are just consuming our attention. Those carefully designed titles, push notifications, and recommendation algorithms do not care whether we grow; what they care about is whether we continue to stay.

People often say that if a service is free, then you are very likely the product itself. Placed in today's context, this sentence is even more accurate than it was back then. What platforms have long been competing for is not content, but users' limited attention. Whoever can make people stay longer will gain more revenue.

The addition of AI has made this competition unprecedentedly fierce. It can learn our habits, analyze our preferences, and remember why we stay, why we click, and why we share. It knows what kind of headlines make it easier for us to open, and what kind of emotions make it easier for us to immerse ourselves. Then, using nearly infinite computing resources, it customizes the most irresistible temptations for everyone.

In such an environment, acquiring information is an easy task, but what truly matters is deciding which information is worth acquiring. Because attention has never been just a way to allocate time; it will ultimately shape a person's thought structure: what a person pays attention to over the long term is what they will slowly become.

If one invests a lot of time every day in anger, anxiety, and emotional arguments, then these emotions will eventually become part of their personality. If one is immersed in fragmented information for a long time, their thinking will gradually become fragmented as well. Conversely, if one invests time in reading, learning, creating, and genuine interpersonal relationships, then these things will also settle down bit by bit, eventually constituting a person's underlying character.

I increasingly feel that many important differences in life are not caused by IQ, but by the flow of attention. Wherever attention flows, that is where life will grow.

Regrettably, those things that truly have long-term value often lack immediate feedback. Reading a book will not instantly change one's destiny, learning a language will not yield returns tomorrow, and sticking to exercise for a few months may not immediately show results. Most of the time, they appear plain, slow, and even somewhat boring.

And those things that most easily attract attention are often exactly the opposite. They are immediate, intense, and stimulating, able to quickly mobilize emotions, but rarely leave any real accumulation.


I don't believe there is a panacea that can solve the attention problem. Most of the time, we still need to rely on self-discipline and willpower, and we need to constantly remind ourselves what the truly important things are. But there are indeed some methods that can help us better manage our attention, such as: less mindless scrolling, more focused reading; less chasing after hot topics, more accumulation of long-term skills; less spectating, more creating. Because creation forces one to organize their thoughts, while consumption only continuously exhausts them. This is true for writing, for programming, and for life as well.

For machines, it is a technological route. For humans, it is more like a survival strategy. Future AI will become increasingly powerful, content will become increasingly abundant, and information will continue to grow exponentially. We cannot decide how much noise the world will produce every day, but we can still decide who to lend our ears to, who to give our time to, and who to entrust our attention to.
