# To My Future Self in the AI Era

> "If you can one day renovate yourself, do so from day to day. Yea, let there be daily renovation." — *The Great Learning*

The recent advancements in AI have truly astounded me. It feels like an approaching tsunami; the more I pay attention, the more anxious I become.

Being in the IT industry, which is notoriously considered a "young man's game," I've always harbored a strong sense of career crisis. This industry evolves too fast—you could be the company's star today and the target for optimization tomorrow. So, years ago, I realized I couldn't possibly work in this industry until retirement, and I seriously pondered what I could do after losing my job.

Two years ago, I still felt there were at least a few paths forward. For example, becoming a training instructor—teaching junior engineers, or even teaching programming to K-12 students. After all, having navigated the tech giants for years, I'd accumulated some experience. At that time, the entire industry was in a "programming for everyone" craze; it seemed that as long as you could code, you would never run out of students.

Or perhaps writing. I used to submit articles to magazines and even earned royalties. Even if I stopped coding, I thought I could still make a living from words. A programmer transitioning to a writer sounded somewhat romantic—researching technology by day and writing by night, becoming a cyberpunk literati in the internet age.

Unfortunately, the emergence of AI has cut off all these fallback plans I had envisioned. The squeeze AI has put on the demand for programmers is already visible to the naked eye. Much of the work that previously required a team of engineers collaborating for weeks can now be completed by one person with AI in just a few days. AI is not only replacing "people who write code," but also "people who teach others how to write code." If future code is primarily generated by AI, how many people will still want to learn programming?

The writing industry experienced this shock even earlier. Since last year, I've constantly heard about the shrinking publishing industry, plummeting royalties, and the reshuffling of independent media. In the past, a writer's biggest competitors were their peers; now, their biggest competitor is a Large Language Model that is online 24/7, never tires, and doesn't require social security benefits. AI can generate a well-structured, eloquently phrased, and even emotionally resonant article in seconds. Consequently, many people have stopped buying books, stopped waiting years for an author to polish a work, and instead turned to AI to "write one on the spot."

It is a deeply absurd feeling: those of us who once made a living from "knowledge" and "creativity" suddenly find that the first thing to be industrialized is creativity itself. If one day I truly lose my job, there probably won't be such a thing as "changing careers"; it will simply be called retirement. (Well, it seems my previous pessimistic expectations were wrong; I really am going to work in the software industry until "retirement.")

But the problem is, I don't feel I've reached the age where I should retire. So, thinking it over, the most realistic plan is to try not to be eliminated. Survive one more day if I can. Since the wave is already here, I might as well learn to surf first.

Thus, I began forcing myself to seriously study AI programming, research Agents, and explore the endless stream of new tools, new paradigms, and new workflows. But the more I learn, the stronger my sense of weightlessness becomes. This era is changing too fast. Many concepts I barely understood yesterday are already outdated today; a framework released in the morning might have someone proposing "its next-generation replacement" by evening.

I feel it's not me learning technology, but technology desperately trying to leave me behind. This also made me realize that my enthusiasm for "learning" has indeed declined significantly lately. It's not just out of laziness, but because when humans face exponential change, they instinctively develop a sense of powerlessness. You just prepare to earnestly climb a mountain, only to find the entire crust is shifting.

Yet, my rationality still tells me that anxiety is actually pointless. Technological waves won't stop because of fear, nor will they decelerate due to nostalgia. Instead of fantasizing every day about "whether AI will replace me," it's better to dive in and see exactly how far it has developed.

And I am someone who needs a clear driving force to truly settle down and learn. So ultimately, I decided to write another book. Not to teach others anything, but to leave a record for myself. To record how I relearn, readapt, and redefine what it means to be a "programmer" in this chaotic, crazy, constantly changing era.

And what you are reading now is the beginning of this book.

## Content of the Book

This book primarily documents the genuine shock and transformation within an ordinary programmer when AI begins to deeply intervene in the act of "creation."

Of course, the book will also include many practical methods for using AI. And these contents may not necessarily be limited to "programming" itself. Because as AI makes programming increasingly cheap, it has begun to slowly seep into everyday life like water and electricity. The boundaries of "programming" are rapidly blurring. Many people who originally had nothing to do with programming have also begun to control software, generate content, and automate work through natural language. In a sense, everyone in the future may "learn to program" in another form.

The pace of AI's development is almost frighteningly fast. Many technical details, tool names, and workflows in this book may become obsolete in a few months; some things that look advanced today may become the jokes of tomorrow. I will continuously adjust and update the content of this book to keep it as fresh as possible.

If you have any thoughts, opinions, or discover any better tools or more interesting directions during your reading, please feel free to let me know. After all, in an era where the speed of change has surpassed the limits of individual cognition, no one truly dares to say they have "already mastered" it.

## The Starting Point

Five years ago, I first tried open-sourcing a book I wrote on GitHub. At that time, I even manually crafted a webpage using JavaScript first. Later, when I discovered mature e-book tools, I thought that was already very convenient.

Three years ago, I tried writing a book on Python programming. That time, AI showed me what true convenience was: a draft that originally would have taken at least three months to complete was finished in less than a month with AI's assistance. At that time, I vaguely felt that something was starting to feel wrong.

And by the time I truly started writing this book, the world had changed once again. This time, the change was so fast it even made me a bit dazed. From the moment I made up my mind to the time the draft of this book actually went online, it took less than an hour.

The entire process went roughly like this:

- First, I made up my mind: to force myself to systematically learn AI programming through the act of "writing a book."
- Then, I asked AI: If I want to truly master AI programming, what technologies, tools, and directions should I learn?
- AI quickly gave me a long study list.
- Next, I asked AI to generate a table of contents for a book based on this list.
- Subsequently, I opened an AI programming tool and told it: Help me create a Docusaurus-based e-book project and integrate the table of contents generated just now.
- A few minutes later, a fully structured, fully paged, content-rich, and functional e-book project appeared on my computer.
- Next, I opened GitHub and created a repository.
- Then I opened Cloudflare, bound the repository, and deployed the website.
- A few minutes later, this book was officially online.

And when AI generated a "decent-looking" complete e-book in just a few minutes, I suddenly felt dazed again. I couldn't help but doubt: Since AI can already accomplish these things so easily, is what I'm doing now still meaningful?

Later, I could only repeatedly remind myself: The purpose of this book is to force me to continuously learn, continuously think, and continuously keep up with this era. Only with this thought did I continue writing.

Strictly speaking, many steps in the above process no longer even require switching back and forth between tools. Today's AI programming tools have already begun to transform into a "unified workbench." Planning, generating, coding, deploying, modifying, debugging... Many processes that previously required multi-person collaboration can now be completed by one person facing a chat box.

And in the main text following this book, I will also include the complete commands, prompts, and actual conversation processes with AI used when creating this book, to help readers fully reproduce this process.

Of course, the draft going online is not something to be proud of in itself. What truly matters is never "generating a book in a few minutes." What truly matters is: In the time to come, can I truly replace these rapidly AI-generated template contents with my own things through learning, practice, and reflection?

Because only the content that has truly been digested, understood, stumbled over, doubted, and rebuilt by oneself can truly be considered "one's own knowledge."
